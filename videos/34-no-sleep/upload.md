# ماذا لو لم تنم عشرة أيام؟ — Short #34

57.2 s, 1080x1920, 30 fps. Global framing.

What it shows, in order:
1. The real 3D Earth spins; a counter runs from day 1 to day 10, "بلا نوم".
2. Night, a room with a window to the city: you sit at the desk with a book; letters fly into your head, two in five fall away grey; the memory bar drops from 100% to 60%.
3. A head in profile with the brain: asleep, blue fluid runs through the folds and carries the gold waste out; awake, brown clumps pile up.
4. Day 2, morning: you pour water into a mug; the screen goes black for 3 seconds with one wide-open, red-veined eye; then the mug has overflowed onto the desk and the floor.
5. A street by day (new buildings and shops): a tired person walks and sneezes; viruses float; a chart: 7 h+ of sleep = 1, under 6 h = 4 times the colds.
6. Day 3, the same street at night: a road sign turns into a dark figure with glowing eyes, coloured shapes float, the buildings ripple; then it all flickers away.
7. Day 10, the room is a mess: you slump at the desk; 100, 93, 86, 79, 72, 65… then a question mark.
8. The 1964 record: the window cycles day and night while the calendar runs to day 11 (264 hours); then you sleep in bed while the clock counts 14 hours; at sunrise you sit up and stretch, fine.
9. Earth with the question mark and the subscribe button.

## Facts (as narrated)

- **One night without sleep: about 40% less ability to store new memories.** After one night of total sleep deprivation, people formed new memories about 40% worse, with less hippocampus activity (Yoo, Hu, Gujar, Jolesz & Walker, "A deficit in the ability to form new human memories without sleep", Nature Neuroscience 10, 385–392, 2007).
- **The brain cleans out its waste during sleep.** In sleep the space between brain cells grows by about 60% and the brain's fluid washes out waste such as beta-amyloid about twice as fast (Xie et al., Science 342, 373–377, 2013, in mice). In people, one night without sleep raised beta-amyloid in the hippocampus by about 5% (Shokri-Kojori et al., PNAS 115, 4483–4488, 2018).
- **Microsleeps of a few seconds, even with the eyes open.** Sleep-deprived people have involuntary lapses of a fraction of a second to several seconds (some up to about 15 s), and they can happen with the eyes open (Goel et al., "Neurocognitive consequences of sleep deprivation", Seminars in Neurology 29, 320–339, 2009). The 3 seconds on screen is an example in that range.
- **Under 6 hours: 4 times the colds.** After being given the cold virus, people who slept under 6 hours a night were 4.2 times more likely to catch a cold than those who slept over 7 hours (Prather et al., "Behaviorally assessed sleep and susceptibility to the common cold", Sleep 38(9), 1353–1359, 2015).
- **Hallucinations from about day 3.** A review of 21 studies found visual distortions after 24–48 hours awake and hallucinations and delusions after about 72 hours (Waters et al., "Severe sleep deprivation causes hallucinations and a gradual progression toward psychosis with increasing time awake", Frontiers in Psychiatry 9, 303, 2018).
- **100 minus 7, stopping at 65.** On the last day of his record, Randy Gardner was asked to subtract 7 from 100 again and again; he stopped at 65 and said he had forgotten what he was doing (William Dement, "The Promise of Sleep", 1999; Ross, American Journal of Psychiatry, 1965).
- **The record: 17 years old, 11 days, then 14 hours of sleep.** In 1963–64, Randy Gardner, a 17-year-old student, stayed awake for 264.4 hours (about 11 days). He then slept about 14 hours 40 minutes and woke up with no lasting harm reported. Guinness no longer accepts sleep-deprivation records because they are dangerous.
- **Global framing:** the room and the street stand for any city; no country is named.
