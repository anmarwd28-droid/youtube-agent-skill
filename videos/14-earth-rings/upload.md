## Facts (approximate, as narrated)
- Rings settle in a planet's equatorial plane and sit inside the Roche limit (about 2.5–3 Earth radii for icy/rocky material); here they span ~1.35–2.25 Earth radii.
- Seen from Baghdad (33°N) they would form an arch across the southern sky, from roughly 10° to 36° above the horizon, visible by day like the Moon.
- At night the sunlit rings would light the ground more than the Moon, and Earth's shadow would darken the part of the arch opposite the Sun.
- The rings' shadow falls on the winter hemisphere, cutting sunlight there (the 2024 study suggests a ring may have helped cool the climate).
- Debris from a ring would rain down as more meteors; ring material crowding low equatorial orbits would make launches and satellites riskier.
- A 2024 study (Tomkins, Martin & Cawood, Earth and Planetary Science Letters) proposes Earth had a ring about 466 million years ago, based on 21 impact craters that formed near the equator of that time. It is a hypothesis, not settled fact.
