## Facts (as narrated)
- **The sky as a clock:** the day is one turn of the Earth (sunrise to sunrise); the Moon's phases repeat every 29.5 days (synodic month, 29.53 days), the origin of the month.
- **Egypt and the Nile:** the Nile flooded once a year; the Egyptian civil calendar had 365 days (12 months of 30 days + 5 extra days), in use by the 3rd millennium BC.
- **No calendar, no farming:** planting at the right season is what farming depends on; "the first cities might never have risen" is speculation, said as "ربّما".
- **Babylon's sixty:** the Babylonians counted in base 60 (sexagesimal); this is where the division of the hour into 60 minutes and the minute into 60 seconds comes from.
- **Ships lost:** on 22 October 1707 a British fleet under Sir Cloudesley Shovell struck the rocks of the Isles of Scilly in bad weather because of navigation errors; four ships and about 1,400–2,000 sailors were lost. Knowing longitude at sea needs an exact clock; the disaster helped lead to the Longitude Act of 1714.
- **Harrison's clock:** John Harrison's sea watch H4 was tested on a voyage to Jamaica in 1761–62 and kept time well enough to fix longitude; it beats five times a second.
- **Railways:** before standard time each town kept its own local solar time (Bristol was about 10 minutes behind London). Railways brought standard time; in 1884 the International Meridian Conference chose Greenwich as the prime meridian, the basis of the world's time zones.
- **GPS:** every GPS satellite carries atomic clocks. Radio signals travel at the speed of light (about 300,000 km/s), so a clock error of one microsecond (a millionth of a second) gives a range error of about 300 m.
- **Maps, phone networks and banks:** phone networks synchronise their transmitters with GPS time, and banks and stock exchanges timestamp transactions to fractions of a second; without exact time they fail.
- **The body clock:** the suprachiasmatic nucleus, a small group of neurons in the hypothalamus just above the optic chiasm, is the brain's master clock; its natural rhythm is about 24 hours (circadian, ≈ 24.2 h in people) and daylight keeps it in step.
