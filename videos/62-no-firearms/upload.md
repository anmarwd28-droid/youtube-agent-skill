## Facts (numbers and sources)
- About 1 billion firearms in the world (1.013 billion at the end of 2017; 857 million held by civilians): Small Arms Survey, "Estimating Global Civilian-Held Firearms Numbers" (2018).
- Gunpowder was found by Chinese alchemists looking for an elixir of long life, in the 9th century CE (a mid-9th-century Daoist text warns that mixing saltpetre, sulphur and honey makes things burst into flame); the first written formula is in the Wujing Zongyao (1044). 850 CE is about 1,175 years ago («نحوَ ألفٍ ومئتي سنة»). Black powder is about 75 % saltpetre, 15 % charcoal, 10 % sulphur (the classic ratio). Fireworks came from the same powder.
- The land walls of Constantinople (the Theodosian Walls) were built in the early 5th century (413 CE) and held until the Ottoman siege of 1453, in which large bronze cannons (including Orban's great bombard) battered them: about 1,040 years («ألفَ سنة»).
- Speeds: a longbow arrow leaves the bow at about 50–60 m/s; a modern rifle bullet at about 850–950 m/s (5.56 mm NATO ≈ 940 m/s, 7.62 mm ≈ 830 m/s). 900 / 60 = 15 («خمسَ عشرةَ مرّة»).
- Training: a skilled longbowman trained for years, often from childhood; a musket could be taught to a recruit in weeks, which is one of the main reasons firearms replaced bows in the 16th–17th centuries.
- Firearms (together with ships and disease) let small European forces conquer and rule large distant lands in the Americas, Africa and Asia from the 16th to the 19th centuries; without them the balance of power and the world map would have been different.
- People fought wars with clubs, spears, bows and swords for thousands of years before gunpowder (e.g. the battle of Megiddo, about 1457 BCE).
- John Wilkinson patented a precise cannon-boring machine in 1774; from 1775–76 his works bored the cylinders of Boulton & Watt's steam engines, which needed an accurate bore to work well (the first commercial Watt engines ran in 1776).
- Interchangeable parts: French gunsmith Honoré Blanc showed muskets with interchangeable locks (1785–90); the US armories (Springfield, Harpers Ferry) developed "armory practice" in the early 1800s, which spread to sewing machines, bicycles and the car industry (the "American system of manufacturing"; D. Hounshell, *From the American System to Mass Production*, 1984).
