55 s, 1080x1920, 30 fps. Global framing.

What it shows, in order:
1. A city street at night. Our Moon, then a second, bigger moon rises (both are the real 3D Moon). People stop and look up.
2. Space view (not to scale): Earth with two orbits, ours at 27 days and the new one at about 10 days, plus a size comparison ("twice as big").
3. The night street under two full moons: blue light, long moon shadows, and a light meter rising to ×5.
4. A coastal town at night under both moons. The sea rises and floods the low streets, with a tide gauge.
5. A total solar eclipse (corona), then a one-year strip with many eclipses.
6. The two orbits tugging at each other and drifting, then the two moons collide.
7. A real hypothesis: a small companion moon slowly merges into our Moon and forms the far-side highlands (Jutzi & Asphaug 2011).
8. The mini-moon 2024 PT5 on a looping temporary path around Earth.
9. Earth with both moons, the question mark and the subscribe button.

## Facts (as narrated)

The scenario is a second moon with the same size and mass as ours, orbiting at half the distance (about 192,000 km).

**Size and orbit**
- At half the distance it looks twice as wide in the sky (about 1° instead of 0.5°).
- Orbital period scales as distance^1.5 (Kepler's third law): 27.3 days / 2^1.5 ≈ 9.7 days, narrated as "about 10 days".

**Moonlight**
- It is the same distance from the Sun, so it is lit the same way. Twice the width means 4 times the area, so 4 times our full moon's light.
- Together with our Moon that is about 5 times today's full-moon light.

**Tides**
- Tidal force scales as mass / distance³. The same mass at half the distance gives 8 times our Moon's tidal effect.
- Today the Moon's tidal effect is about 2.2 times the Sun's, so total tides would be several times higher. Narrated as "several times".

**Eclipses**
- Today there are 2–5 solar eclipses a year.
- A moon that looks twice as wide and orbits about every 10 days would cover the Sun far more often.

**Long-term stability**
- Two large moons would perturb each other's orbits, and tides slowly move moons outward.
- Over millions of years the orbits could become unstable and the moons might collide. Narrated with "قد" (may).

**The real hypothesis**
- Jutzi & Asphaug, *Nature* 476 (2011), "Forming the lunar farside highlands by accretion of a companion moon".
- A smaller companion moon, about 1,200 km across, may have shared the Moon's orbit, then collided slowly (about 2–3 km/s). The impact would have piled up the far-side highlands.
- This is a hypothesis, not established fact.

**The real mini-moon**
- Asteroid 2024 PT5, about 10 m across, was temporarily captured by Earth's gravity from 29 September to 25 November 2024: about two months.
