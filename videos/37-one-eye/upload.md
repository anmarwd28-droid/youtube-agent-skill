## Facts (as narrated)

- **Two eyes see two slightly different pictures (binocular disparity), and the brain compares them to judge distance (stereopsis).** This works best at close range, for reaching, grasping and catching (Howard & Rogers, "Perceiving in Depth", 2012).
- **With one eye, fine depth tasks get harder:** people who lose an eye, or cover one, do worse at catching, pouring and threading tasks, especially at first (e.g. Servos et al., Experimental Brain Research 2000; Melmoth & Grant, 2006).
- **Field of view about 200° → about 150°.** The horizontal field of both eyes together is about 200° (up to 210°); one eye covers about 150–160° (about 60° toward the nose and 90–100° to the side). Narrated as "نحو مئتي درجة… إلى نحو مئة وخمسين".
- **No spare eye.** With one eye, one injury to it means total blindness; with two, the other eye still sees.
- **The brain uses one-eye depth cues:** motion parallax (moving the head makes near things slide faster than far ones), relative size, shadows, overlap and perspective. People who lose an eye adapt with these cues.
- **3D films need two eyes.** Stereoscopic 3D shows a different image to each eye (polarised or red/cyan glasses); with one eye there is no 3D effect.
- **The cyclops myth and dwarf elephant skulls.** The palaeontologist Othenio Abel suggested in 1914 that skulls of dwarf elephants found on Mediterranean islands (Sicily, Crete, Cyprus, Malta) inspired the one-eyed giant: the large central nasal opening looks like one big eye socket, while the real eye sockets are small and at the sides. It is a hypothesis ("ربّما"), discussed by Adrienne Mayor, "The First Fossil Hunters", 2000.
- **Cyclops, a real one-eyed animal.** Cyclops is a genus of tiny freshwater copepods (about 0.5–3 mm long). It has a single median (naupliar) eye, a red spot in the middle of the head, and females carry two egg sacs.
- **Global framing:** the street and the people stand for anyone in the world.
