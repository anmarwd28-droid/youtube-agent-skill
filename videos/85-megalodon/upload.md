## Facts (numbers and sources)
- Otodus megalodon lived from the early Miocene, about 23 million years ago, to the early Pliocene; the best current extinction date is about 3.6 million years ago (Boessenecker et al., PeerJ 2019; earlier estimate ~2.6 Ma by Pimiento & Clements, PLoS ONE 2014). It is the largest shark known.
- Fossil teeth are found worldwide in tropical to temperate seas, on every continent except Antarctica (Pimiento et al., J. Biogeography 2016). Sites shown: e.g. North Carolina and Florida (USA), Panama, Peru, Chile, Venezuela, Baja California, Malta, Spain, Morocco, India, Japan, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Java.
- Length: estimates range about 15–20 m for the largest individuals (Shimada 2019, Historical Biology, ~15 m; Cooper et al., Sci. Rep. 2020, ~16 m; Perez et al., Palaeontologia Electronica 2021, up to ~20 m). Drawn at 18 m. A city bus is about 12 m; the great white reaches about 6 m. "Three times the great white" = 18 ÷ 6 (rounded, «نحو»).
- Body shape: a 2024 study found it was probably more slender than a scaled-up great white (Sternes et al., Palaeontologia Electronica 2024); the drawing uses a slightly slimmer body than the great white.
- Teeth: slant height up to about 18 cm (7 in); an adult hand is about 18–19 cm long, so «قد تصل أسنانه إلى طول يدك». Great white teeth are about 5–6 cm.
- Bite force: 108,514–182,201 N (Wroe et al., Journal of Zoology 2008); great white about 18,200 N in the same study (6.4 m shark); human maximum bite about 1,300 N. 182,000 ÷ 18,200 ≈ 10 → «حتى عشرة أضعاف».
- Diet: whales and other marine mammals; bite marks on fossil whale bones and flippers (e.g. Godfrey et al. 2018; Collareta et al. 2017).
- Nurseries: young megalodon grew up in shallow, warm coastal waters (Pimiento et al., PLoS ONE 2010, Gatun Formation, Panama; Herraiz et al. 2020). Newborns were about 2 m long (Shimada et al., Historical Biology 2021).
- Today's oceans are colder than in the Miocene–Pliocene; megalodon fossils are linked to warm and temperate waters, so a modern population would likely keep to warm seas (said with «ربّما»).
- Extinction causes discussed: Pliocene ocean cooling, decline and change in prey (baleen whales), and competition with the smaller great white (Boessenecker et al. 2019; McCormack et al., Nature Communications 2022, zinc isotopes; Kast et al. 2022).
