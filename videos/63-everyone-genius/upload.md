## Facts (numbers and sources)
- IQ tests are normed to a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15 (Wechsler, Stanford–Binet). Above 130 is 2 standard deviations: about 2.3 % of people («نحوَ اثنينِ بالمئة»).
- World population about 8.1–8.2 billion in 2025 (UN World Population Prospects 2024) («ثمانيةُ ملياراتِ»).
- Einstein's brain weighed 1,230 g at autopsy (Harvey, 1955; Witelson et al., *The Lancet* 1999), below the average adult male brain of about 1,350–1,400 g. Brain size explains only a small part of IQ differences (correlation about 0.2–0.3; Pietschnig et al. 2015).
- The brain is about 2 % of body mass and uses about 20 % of the body's resting energy, about 20 W (Raichle & Gusnard, PNAS 2002).
- About 8.8 million full-time-equivalent researchers worldwide in 2018 (UNESCO Science Report 2021) («نحوُ تسعةِ ملايينِ باحث»).
- Higher childhood intelligence is linked to longer life: in a meta-analysis, one standard deviation higher IQ ≈ 24 % lower risk of death over follow-up (Calvin et al., *Int. J. Epidemiology* 2011; Deary, "cognitive epidemiology").
- Intelligence does not stop disagreement: people with higher numeracy can be more polarised on politicised questions ("motivated numeracy", Kahan et al., *Behavioural Public Policy* 2017).
- IQ is relative: tests are re-normed every few years so the average is set back to 100.
- The Flynn effect: average IQ test scores rose about 3 points per decade in the 20th century in many countries (Flynn 1984, 1987; Pietschnig & Voracek, meta-analysis 2015: about 2.8 points per decade). Flynn: the average person around 1900, scored against today's norms, would get about 70. In some countries the rise has slowed or reversed since the 1990s, so the narration says "in the 20th century".
